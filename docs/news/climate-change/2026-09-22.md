### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> EPA to Remove Emissions Limits on Climate Pollution From Power Plants
The EPA announced it will eliminate emissions limits on greenhouse gases from coal and natural gas power plants, reversing Biden-era rules requiring 90% carbon capture. EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin claimed the move will save $310 billion in compliance costs, but the decision will result in 123–136 million metric tons of additional carbon emissions over the next decade.

<ul class="takeaways">
<li>EPA eliminating greenhouse gas emissions standards for power plants</li>
<li>Reverses Biden rules requiring 90% carbon capture for coal and new natural gas plants</li>
<li>EPA claims $310 billion in compliance cost savings (Guardian reported $370M)</li>
<li>Will add 123–136 million metric tons CO₂ over next decade</li>
<li>EPA claims power plant emissions have &#x27;no material impact&#x27; on global climate change</li>
</ul>

## Climate & Health

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> Climate drought and disease crisis among Matsés Indigenous people in Peru
The recently contacted Matsés people in Peru's Amazon face a severe health emergency from drought, epidemic disease, and healthcare access gaps. Climate-driven low river levels prevent boat transit, blocking healthcare delivery. Advanced hepatitis B and malaria cases are causing deaths in isolated villages.

<ul class="takeaways">
<li>Hepatitis B cases identified: civil society brigades found 17 cases (~20% population screening)</li>
<li>Advanced hepatitis B complications including cirrhosis causing deaths</li>
<li>Climate drought has lowered river levels, restricting boat navigation (primary transit in roadless region)</li>
<li>Ministry of Health cited budget constraints and geographic remoteness as reasons for delays</li>
<li>Forced displacement due to disease or drought could expose Indigenous people to illegal oil, logging, and mining</li>
</ul>

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> Rising Sea Levels Pose Existential Threat to Humans, Heritage, and Nature
Rising sea levels are reshaping economies and cultures for 770 million people—10% of the world's population—living in acute risk zones. Global oceans rose 20 cm from 1901–2018 and 10.6 cm since 1993; under moderate-emissions scenarios, they will rise an additional 56 cm by 2100, with extreme sea level events increasing 12-fold since 1900.

<ul class="takeaways">
<li>770 million people (10% of global population) in acute risk (&lt;5m above high-tide line)</li>
<li>20 cm rise 1901–2018; 10.6 cm rise since 1993</li>
<li>Predicted additional 56 cm rise by 2100 under moderate-emissions scenario</li>
<li>100-year extreme sea level events increased 12-fold since 1900</li>
<li>Affects major cities: Mumbai, Kolkata, Shenzhen, Guangzhou; entire island nations threatened</li>
</ul>

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> Copper mining boom for energy transition threatens Andean wildlands and vicuña habitat
An $18 billion copper mining project named Vicuña in the high Andes threatens fragile ecosystems and the vicuña, an Indigenous-revered species. Global copper demand is expected to reach 50 million metric tons by 2040—nearly double 2026 consumption of 28 million metric tons—driven by renewable energy technologies, forcing mining expansion into untapped wildlands and Indigenous territories.

<ul class="takeaways">
<li>$18 billion Vicuña project straddles Argentina (San Juan) and Chile (Atacama)</li>
<li>Global copper demand projected: 50 million metric tons by 2040 vs. 28 million in 2026</li>
<li>Vicuña population recovered from 6,000 (1974) to ~350,000 today through legal protections</li>
<li>Mining expansion threatens fragile Andean ecosystems and Indigenous lands</li>
<li>Mineral extraction identified as key driver of 95% Latin American wildlife decline 1970–2020</li>
</ul>

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> Poorest Nations Face Last-Minute Climate Benefits Even in Net-Zero Scenario
Australian climate researchers found that tropical and economically disadvantaged regions would remain among the last to experience climate benefits even if global emissions reach net-zero by 2050. The inequality in climate impact would not reverse under net-zero scenarios and could take "many decades to begin to reverse" even under net-negative emissions plans.

<ul class="takeaways">
<li>Tropical regions warmed more relative to historic range than higher latitudes</li>
<li>Wealthier countries caused more warming but remain less exposed</li>
<li>Climate inequality persists indefinitely under net-zero path</li>
<li>Reversal could take &#x27;many decades&#x27; under net-negative emissions</li>
<li>Research from University of Melbourne; published in Environmental Research Letters</li>
</ul>
