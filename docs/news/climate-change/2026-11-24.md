### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> COP31 Negotiations Collapse Without Climate Agreement
The 31st UN Climate Change Conference (COP31) collapsed on November 23, 2026, in Antalya, Türkiye, without adopting an overarching political agreement after nearly 200 nations failed to reach consensus. The impasse ended the longest UN climate summit on record after more than 49 hours of overtime negotiations, marking the first failure to formally adopt a political pact since the 2009 Copenhagen climate conference (COP15). COP31 President Murat Kurum ruled the proposed final text could not be adopted after the European Union, the United Kingdom, Norway, and Panama rejected draft language omitting any reference to transitioning away from fossil fuels. Major oil-producing states, including Saudi Arabia and Russia, opposed naming fossil fuels in the pact. Developing nations such as Kenya and Ghana criticized inadequate financial support and the removal of a proposed $120 billion adaptation finance target. The United States sent no official delegation for the second consecutive year following its withdrawal from the Paris Agreement. UN Secretary-General António Guterres stated in an official statement that while there is no consensus in the negotiating room, there is no ambiguity in the science, and every delay makes the climate task harder and more costly. Despite the collapse of the overarching political pact, parties still adopted technical decisions advancing a just transition framework, climate finance, and adaptation. Voluntary initiatives included pledges from 150 countries backing a target of 35 percent global electrification by 2035. The 2023 UAE Consensus from COP28, which calls on nations to transition away from fossil fuels, remains in effect. Parties will reconvene at COP32 in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, next year to resume negotiations.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 8/10" aria-label="Importance 8 of 10">8</span> England Records 5,258 Summer Heat-Related Deaths in 2026, Nearly Double Previous Peak
England recorded 5,258 heat-related deaths between May and September 2026, according to the UK Health Security Agency, the highest provisional annual total since records began in 2016. This nearly doubles the previous peak of 2,985 deaths in 2022. The summer included seven heat episodes spanning 41 total days. The deadliest period was June 21-28, with an estimated 2,401 deaths. Temperatures reached 38°C in Norfolk in June and 38.2°C in Gloucestershire in August. England also experienced its driest July on record with 7mm of rain. The June heatwave triggered only the second red heat-health alert ever issued in England. Prior annual totals were 2,295 deaths in 2023, 1,311 in 2024, and 1,504 in 2025. UKHSA's principal environmental health scientist stated that climate change is already affecting people's health in England, and extreme heat periods are expected to become longer, more frequent, and more intense as the climate continues to warm.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Kiwi Birds Return to Wellington Hills After 150 Years in Māori-Led Conservation
More than 250 kiwi have been reintroduced to the hills surrounding Wellington, New Zealand, marking the first wild population in the region in over 150 years. The reintroduction, led by the Capital Kiwi project in partnership with Indigenous iwi (Māori nations), local communities, and private landowners, began in late 2022. Project teams deployed more than 5,300 traps across 24,000 hectares of farmland, forestry, and suburban reserves to control stoats (Mustela erminea), the primary predator threat to young kiwi. Under the project's predator management regime, chick survival rates have exceeded 90%, compared to nearly complete mortality in unmanaged areas. Including wild-hatched chicks, the local population is estimated to exceed 300 birds. The reintroduced birds are reported to be in good condition and laying eggs. The translocations followed traditional Māori protocols, including formal gifting requests (tono) and welcoming ceremonies (pōwhiri). Project founder Paul Ward and Kaimanaaki Kiwi (kiwi guardian) Rawiri Walsh emphasized that the initiative reflects Māori values of stewardship and restoration of treasured species (taonga) to the whenua (land).

## Geopolitics

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> War disrupts global food system reliance on fossil fuels and sulfur production
The Iran war has shuttered Persian Gulf refineries, disrupting sulfur production and threatening global food supplies. Sulfur, a byproduct of oil and natural gas refining, is the feedstock for sulfuric acid, which is essential for processing phosphate rock into phosphate fertilizer. The conflict has reduced phosphate fertilizer supplies by 40 percent and raised prices beyond the reach of many farmers. Over 95 percent of the world's sulfur now comes from oil and natural gas refineries; nearly half of internationally traded sulfur previously came from Persian Gulf refineries before supplies were cut off in 2026. This dependency traces to acid rain prevention efforts over 30 years ago that required refineries to strip sulfur from their products, making waste sulfur the go-to feedstock after almost all traditional sulfur mines shut down. Analysts expect the crisis will cut crop yields in 2027 and beyond, raising food prices globally for years. The World Food Programme warns that combined with El Niño, the shortage could bring hunger to at least an additional 49 million people. As the world phases out fossil fuels, the loss of waste sulfur from refineries poses a broader threat to fertilizer production and demand for sulfur in battery manufacturing and metal ore refining.
