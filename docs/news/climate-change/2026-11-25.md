## Climate

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 8.5/10" aria-label="Importance 8.5 of 10">8.5</span> World records six consecutive days at 2°C above preindustrial temperatures
For the first time, the world experienced six consecutive days of global surface temperatures at least 2°C above preindustrial levels (baseline 1850–1900). The daily global surface temperature reached 2.24°C above preindustrial levels on Monday, November 24, 2026—the highest ever recorded—and the streak is forecast to continue for at least several more days. Previous occasions when daily temperatures exceeded 2°C occurred in November 2023 and February 2024, but neither lasted as long or reached as high. The previous single-day record was 2.05°C, set in 2023. Scientists note this six-day streak is too brief to signify the upper Paris climate agreement target has been breached, but Samantha Burgess of the Copernicus Climate Change Service described it as "an exceptional global temperature anomaly" and an early indication of warming to come unless fossil fuel emissions are rapidly eliminated. The temperature spikes are driven by long-term anthropogenic heating and an unusually large El Niño approaching its peak, comparable in scale to the 1870s El Niño. Zeke Hausfather of Berkeley Earth noted the new record has "smashed" the previous 2.05°C baseline.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> Cargill publicly supports EU deforestation law while lobbying to weaken it
Cargill, one of the world's largest agribusiness companies and a major trader of soy, beef, palm oil, and cocoa, publicly states it is prepared to comply with the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) scheduled to take effect December 30, 2026 for large and medium operators. The EUDR requires companies to prove certain commodities are not sourced from land deforested after 2020. However, according to an investigation by Earthsight published November 10, 2026, Cargill has simultaneously been lobbying behind the scenes to weaken the law and delay implementation. The company has met with European Commission officials and staff in 2025 and 2026, raising concerns about food shortages caused by the EUDR's "regulatory complexity" and "practical challenges with compliance and legality requirements." Cargill has used a network of trade associations, parliamentary forums, business chambers, and public affairs consultancies to push for changes to the law. The company also held meetings with members of the European Parliament including Renew MEP Benoît Cassart and European People's Party MEP Stefan Köhler, who have since been publicly critical of the EUDR. In 2025, Cargill stated it had mapped its direct suppliers in Argentina, Brazil, and Uruguay and committed that all soy from those countries would be deforestation- and conversion-free by the end of 2025.

## Environment

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> U.S. Data Centers Driving Pollution That Causes Hundreds of Early Deaths
U.S. data centers are driving increased power demand that results in greater pollution from coal and gas power plants, with researchers estimating hundreds of premature deaths annually. A modeling study published in Environmental Research Letters found that in 2023, the added power demand from data centers led to an estimated 650 early deaths across the U.S. The study's authors note that given the rapid expansion of data centers, the impact would likely be even greater in subsequent years. Pollution impacts extend far from data center locations due to regional power grid connections; for example, data centers in Virginia can spur power plants in the Midwest to increase output, raising particulate pollution linked to heart attacks, strokes, and lung disease. A separate study published in Nature Sustainability examined more than 400 hyperscale data centers across the U.S. and found they consume as much electricity as 8 million homes. In Oregon and Virginia, two data center hotspots, the facilities consume more than 15 percent of the electricity sold in each state.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Severe El Niño Drought Dries Up Amazon Rivers, Cuts Off Communities in Brazil
A third major El Niño-fueled drought since 2015 has severely impacted Roraima, Brazil's northernmost Amazonian state. As of October 2026, 95% of the state experienced severe drought conditions, up from 47% in September, according to Brazil's federal water agency (ANA). The 775-kilometer Branco River, Roraima's main waterway, has dwindled to critical levels, exposing large stretches of sand and allowing people to walk beneath the Macuxis Bridge in state capital Boa Vista. Two upstream tributaries feeding the Branco—the Tacutu and Maú rivers—dropped earlier and faster than typical. On November 10, ANA declared a critical water shortage for both rivers through April 30, 2027, affecting Indigenous territories including Raposa Serra do Sol and Jacamim. The Branco River Basin received approximately 390 millimeters (15.4 inches) less rain than the historical average during the 12-month period from October 2025 to September 2026. Between November 16 and 22, disaster response teams delivered supplies to seven isolated villages along the lower Branco using shallow-draft boats. In Caicubi village, which has no road or airstrip, residents were stranded for approximately two weeks. In Panacarica, the river dried up entirely, forcing residents to walk up to 2 kilometers to fetch water. Climate scientist Regina Rodrigues from the Federal University of Santa Catarina attributed the drought to a strong 2026-27 El Niño event and noted that strong El Niño occurrences are becoming more frequent: historically occurring every 15 years, the interval compressed to three years between the 2023-24 and 2026-27 events, a pattern she attributes to anthropogenic climate change.
