## Climate Change

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 8.5/10" aria-label="Importance 8.5 of 10">8.5</span> 1.5°C warming target declared unachievable; climate policy in crisis
The United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) declared in September 2026 that the 1.5°C global warming target set by the Paris Agreement is no longer achievable. The world has already warmed approximately 1.4°C above pre-industrial levels. UNEP's 2026 Emissions Gap Report states that breaching the 1.5°C threshold is now imminent and inevitable. To remain under 1.5°C, total emissions from 2026 onward cannot exceed 130 billion tonnes of CO2, but with current emissions at roughly 40 billion tonnes per year and still rising, this carbon budget will be exhausted in approximately three years. The only remaining pathway identified by UNEP would require global CO2 emissions to fall steadily from 2025 to zero by 2031—described as infeasible under current policies. Under current trajectories, the world is headed for 2.6°C warming by century's end. UNEP's new focus is limiting the peak and duration of overshoot, with the goal of returning global temperatures to 1.5°C by the end of the century. This would require global greenhouse gas emissions reductions of 18% by 2030, 45% by 2035, and 69% by 2040 relative to 2025 levels. However, even with full implementation of governments' nationally determined contributions (NDCs), emissions would only be reduced by 5–8% by 2030 and 11–15% by 2035. At the COP31 climate summit in Turkey in November 2026, nations remain divided on how to respond. Some countries, including vulnerable Pacific states like Palau, are pushing to maintain 1.5°C as the "right goal" and explore returning to it. Other developing nations, including members of the Arab Group and Like-Minded Developing Countries (which includes Saudi Arabia and India), are reportedly seeking to move away from the 1.5°C target, fearing stronger commitments could increase pressure to cut emissions without additional financial support. UNEP Executive Director Inger Andersen stated that exceeding 1.5°C would bring intensified extreme weather, loss of small island states, and growing risks to human health, food, and water security.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 8/10" aria-label="Importance 8 of 10">8</span> Clean Energy Transition Drives Massive Land Reorganization, Threatening Indigenous Rights
According to a November 2026 report from the International Land Coalition (ILC) and Land Matrix, commercial concessions for clean energy infrastructure and critical transition minerals span an estimated 328 million hectares (810.5 million acres) globally—an area equivalent to the size of India. Large-scale solar, wind, hydro, and geothermal facilities account for 159 million hectares (393 million acres), while mining concessions for critical minerals like copper, lithium, nickel, and cobalt cover 239 million hectares (591 million acres). Projects in early exploration and planning phases represent 80% of the total mapped area, with only roughly 20% of mapped projects currently operational. Half of all transition mineral concessions (approximately 120 million hectares or 297 million acres) and 25% of utility-scale renewable energy projects overlap directly with Indigenous Peoples' and local communities' lands. The report notes that an electric vehicle requires six times the mineral inputs of a conventional car, and onshore wind requires nine times more minerals than a natural gas plant. The report warns that the energy transition threatens to reproduce destructive patterns of traditional extractivism under a 'green banner' without strict land safeguards.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> Global Debt Crisis Deepens Climate Vulnerability in Poor Countries Ahead of COP31
Poor countries are redirecting climate adaptation and development funds toward debt repayment, deepening their vulnerability to climate disasters ahead of COP31. Nepal's August glacier collapse and flooding killed thousands and cost an estimated $5 billion in reconstruction, yet the Loss and Damage Fund allocated only $20 million to Nepal—less than 5% of the $482 million Kathmandu must pay external creditors in 2026. ActionAid's September report 'Debt Fuels the Climate Crisis' found that global south external borrowing is 100 times greater than climate finance received. Between 2021 and 2024, debt of developing nations (excluding China) rose 2% but debt repayments increased 28%. External interest costs in the global south reached $125 billion in 2024. Rising US interest rates have increased borrowing costs for developing nations in dollar-denominated debt, forcing them to service loans while pressured to export fossil fuels rather than invest in green industrialization, creating what UN climate chief Simon Stiell called a 'downward loop' where climate risks worsen financial capacity.

## Conservation

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> Scimitar-Horned Oryx Successfully Reintroduced to Chad After Extinction in the Wild
The scimitar-horned oryx, an antelope species with distinctive sword-like horns, disappeared from Chad by the late 1980s and was declared extinct in the wild globally in 2000. A reintroduction program led by the NGO Sahara Conservation has successfully restored the species to Chad, improving its conservation status from extinct in the wild to "endangered." The oryx population in Ouadi Rimé–Ouadi Achim Wildlife Reserve, Chad's largest protected area, has grown from 25 individuals in 2016 to 700 as of November 2026, making it the largest herd in the world. The species continues to face challenges including climate change-driven prolonged droughts, shifting environmental conditions, and competition from livestock for grazing lands.
