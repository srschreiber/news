## Climate Intervention

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Targeted marine cloud brightening demonstrated to weaken subsequent El Niño
UC San Diego researchers demonstrated that marine cloud brightening—spraying sea salt particles to increase cloud reflectivity—could significantly weaken El Niño events when applied strategically over the southeastern Pacific Ocean. Climate simulations show the technique disrupts ocean-atmosphere feedbacks, though researchers note potential risks for other regions including Europe and Asia.

<ul class="takeaways">
<li>Marine cloud brightening consistently weakened simulated El Niño events in climate models</li>
<li>Technique sprays sea salt particles to bounce sunlight back to space</li>
<li>Targeted application during early El Niño development most effective</li>
<li>Current El Niño forecasted to peak 3–4°C above average sea surface temperature</li>
<li>Approach controversial with potential unintended regional consequences</li>
</ul>

## Health

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> Wildfire smoke now exceeds human-source air pollution as prenatal threat
Wildfire smoke has become a greater prenatal health threat than regulated human-source air pollution in the United States, according to a University of Maryland study of nearly 65 million births (2003–2019). While human emissions fell 37% over 16 years, wildfire smoke contributions doubled, erasing decades of air quality gains and disproportionately affecting low-income, rural, and Indigenous populations.

<ul class="takeaways">
<li>Human-source PM2.5 prenatal exposure decreased 37% over 16-year period</li>
<li>Wildfire smoke contributions doubled, offsetting those gains</li>
<li>Prenatal wildfire smoke exposure increases risk of premature birth and low birth weight</li>
<li>Low-income, rural, Indigenous populations bore disproportionate burden</li>
</ul>
