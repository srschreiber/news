### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Extreme wildfire spread accelerating ecosystem change in North American forests
A study published in Science Advances examined nearly 3,500 wildfires across western North America conifer forests between 2012 and 2023. Researchers from Colorado State University, Western Colorado University, UC Berkeley, the U.S. Forest Service, the Canadian Forest Service, and others analyzed more than 33,000 individual daily wildfire spread events. The study found that fast-moving wildfires burn more severely than slower fires, leaving fewer surviving trees to reseed burned landscapes. As fire spread rates increase, the amount of forest experiencing near-total tree mortality increases, as does the distance to surviving trees capable of reseeding. Many western tree species depend on nearby living trees to naturally repopulate burned areas. When severe fires leave large expanses without seed trees, forests have difficulty recovering and may transition to shrublands, grasslands, or other vegetation types better suited to warmer, drier conditions rather than recovering as forests. Lead author Jonathan Coop noted that fire speed is as important as fire size in determining forest outcomes. The research indicates that as conditions become more favorable for fast fire growth, forests may increasingly experience long-term ecological change rather than recovery. A separate Nature Communications study on eight European mountain regions found forest fires have spread upward approximately 72 meters per decade since 2000, with fire occurrence increasing 30% per year below 1,400 meters elevation and 12% above that elevation after 2015. Changes in burned area in those regions are primarily driven by climate factors including vapor pressure deficit, soil moisture, and precipitation, with fires above 1,400 meters linked to extreme atmospheric aridity.
