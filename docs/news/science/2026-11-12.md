### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Europe's Hera spacecraft inspects asteroid struck by NASA's 2022 planetary defense test
The European Space Agency's Hera spacecraft has begun close-range observations of the Didymos binary asteroid system, located 197 million kilometers from Earth. Hera captured its first close-up images in November 2026, taken from 60 kilometers away using its monochromatic Asteroid Framing Camera. The system consists of the larger asteroid Didymos (approximately 780 meters in diameter) and its moonlet Dimorphos (approximately 151 meters in diameter). Hera is now conducting observations from 25–30 kilometers away and will approach to approximately 20 kilometers in the coming month. The mission investigates the aftermath of NASA's DART (Double Asteroid Redirection Test) spacecraft, which deliberately struck Dimorphos in September 2022 at approximately 6.1 kilometers per second. The 580-kilogram DART spacecraft's impact shortened Dimorphos's orbital period around Didymos by approximately 33 minutes—a change greater than previously predicted—and nudged the moonlet into a slightly different path around the Sun. The impact ejected rubble and boulders, with a debris trail extending more than 10,000 kilometers into space for months. Hera carries 25 science instruments and will deploy two shoebox-sized CubeSats, Milani and Juventas, to conduct mineralogical analysis and ground-penetrating radar surveys of the asteroid's subsurface structure. Hera's investigations aim to improve scientific understanding of asteroid impacts and strengthen future planetary defense efforts. Detailed images of both asteroids were scheduled for release on November 25, 2026.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Lumina Supercomputer Simulation Reveals Universe's Emergence from Cosmic Dark Age
Lumina is a supercomputer simulation developed by researchers at MIT, Harvard University, and other institutions that recreates 2 billion years of early cosmic history across a region approximately 1.6 billion light-years wide (500 megaparsecs). The simulation tracks dark matter, gas, stars, and radiation, revealing how the universe transitioned from the cosmic dark age—the period beginning around 380,000 years after the Big Bang when the universe became transparent but stars had not yet formed—to a cosmos filled with galaxies and stars. Lumina captures two major ionization events: hydrogen reionization and helium reionization, showing how the first stars, galaxies, and black holes produced radiation that gradually ionized neutral hydrogen and helium, making the universe transparent to light. The research was published in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society (MNRAS) in November 2026 and provides details that previous simulations could not achieve.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Earth's water may have formed in planet's early atmosphere through chemical reactions
Scientists at the University of New Mexico discovered that rocky pebbles reacting with hydrogen under extreme heat can produce water rapidly—in minutes—potentially generating hundreds of oceans' worth during Earth's formation. The research, led by graduate student Susmita Garai with professors Zachary Sharp and Peter Olson, examined what happens when iron-containing rocks react with hydrogen at extremely high temperatures, mimicking conditions in Earth's early atmosphere. The study, published in Nature Geoscience, suggests that water may have been produced directly through chemical reactions during pebble accretion—the process by which small rock pieces gradually came together to form Earth—rather than being delivered entirely by icy comets or asteroids from distant regions of the early solar system. The findings indicate that water formation may be a natural part of how rocky planets develop, potentially making habitable worlds more common throughout the universe than previously thought.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> US produce and nut crops increasingly depend on unsustainable groundwater depletion
A new study finds that approximately half of the groundwater used to irrigate American specialty crops—including almonds, walnuts, grapes, and other produce—comes from aquifers being depleted faster than they naturally recharge, making current irrigation practices unsustainable. The finding highlights a critical vulnerability in U.S. agricultural production, particularly in California's Central Valley, which produces over 250 crops valued at approximately $17 billion annually and contributes an estimated 25 percent of the nation's food supply. Key crops affected include almonds, pistachios, table grapes, wine grapes, walnuts, and other specialty products. Farmers have increasingly relied on groundwater extraction to compensate for declining surface water availability due to drought conditions exacerbated by climate change. During drought years, farmers pump millions of acre-feet of groundwater in excess of natural aquifer recharge rates—for example, one water expert estimated California farmers would pump an additional 6 to 7 million acre-feet above normal use in a single drought year, a volume that far exceeds annual aquifer replenishment even during normal rainfall years. Some individual farmers report that 90 to 95 percent of their annual crop water demands are being met by groundwater extraction. This unsustainable depletion pattern affects not only food production but also rural communities and disadvantaged populations dependent on groundwater resources.
