### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 8.5/10" aria-label="Importance 8.5 of 10">8.5</span> Anthropic researcher announces AI-assisted proof of Riemann hypothesis
Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, announced on November 20, 2026, that he has discovered a proof of the Riemann hypothesis using Anthropic's Claude AI. The Riemann hypothesis, first proposed in 1859 by German mathematician Bernhard Riemann, is one of mathematics' most famous unsolved problems and is worth a $1 million Clay Mathematics Institute Millennium Prize. The hypothesis proposes that prime numbers follow a specific pattern along the number line. Alpöge's Claude-assisted proof runs to more than 250,000 pages and has been formalized in Lean, a computer programming language used to mechanically verify proofs. The Lean code verification is over 17 million lines long. The proof was subsequently verified by independent researchers. Alpöge stated he also believes the proof confirms the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, a broader expansion of Riemann's conjecture. The announcement caps a year during which AI systems from OpenAI and Anthropic made breakthroughs on numerous open mathematical problems, including OpenAI's solutions to the Navier-Stokes problem and over 700 papers claiming solutions to 372 open problems. This announcement came one day after Anthropic's stock market debut.

## Cosmology

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 8.5/10" aria-label="Importance 8.5 of 10">8.5</span> Ancient quasar pair from cosmic dawn defies galaxy formation models
Astronomers discovered Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua (officially J1602+4228AB), the oldest known compact pair of quasars, existing 900 million years after the Big Bang. The two black holes powering the quasars have masses of 1.5 billion and 400 million solar masses respectively. The pair holds the record for the closest separation between two known quasars from cosmic dawn. Their rapid growth to such massive sizes in the early universe contradicts current galaxy formation models, which cannot explain how black holes became so large so quickly. The discovery combined observations from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and the Gemini North telescope on Maunakea, Hawaii. Research was led by Silvia Onorato, a postdoctoral research associate at NSF NOIRLab, and published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters. Paired quasars are theoretically expected to be relatively common due to frequent galaxy encounters and mergers, but finding and studying them is exceptionally difficult, making this close pair from such an early cosmic epoch particularly remarkable.

## History

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Archaeologists reveal Iron Age British ruler Verica may have been a woman
Archaeologists from Bournemouth University have presented evidence that Verica, the Iron Age ruler whose exile around AD 40 triggered Rome's invasion of Britain in AD 43, was female rather than male as traditionally assumed. Verica ruled the Atrebates tribe across Hampshire and West Sussex. After being forced from power, Verica fled to Rome and appealed to Emperor Claudius for assistance; Roman historian Cassius Dio recorded that this appeal motivated Claudius to invade Britain. Dr. Miles Russell, Director of Archaeological Fieldwork at Bournemouth University, identifies three lines of evidence for Verica's female identity: the name Verica ends in "a," consistent with female names of the era (similar to Cartimandua and Boudica); coins issued under Verica's rule bear the Latin title "rex," which Russell argues is gender-neutral and was used by both male and female rulers recognized by Rome, including Cleopatra of Egypt; and artistic impressions depict Verica in Iron Age dress with Roman-style accessories including gold jewelry and a Julio-Claudian hairstyle. Russell attributes the historical misidentification to the patriarchal bias of 19th-century classical historians.
