### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> DESI Provides Most Precise Measurement of Milky Way Mass
The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) survey has produced the most precise measurement of the Milky Way's mass by analyzing 10,036 distant halo stars—the largest sample used for this purpose. Using data from DESI Data Release 2 and Gaia DR3, researchers tracked two types of stellar tracers: RR Lyrae stars (pulsating stars with accurately measurable distances) and Blue horizontal branch (BHB) stars (bright stars visible at great distances). These stars span distances from roughly 10,000 to 95,000 parsecs (approximately 33,000 to 310,000 light-years) from the Galactic Center. The team employed four different dynamical modeling approaches and generated mock surveys from cosmological simulations to assess potential biases and correct for systematic effects. The study determined that the Milky Way contains 6.36 × 10¹¹ solar masses within 80 kiloparsecs (approximately 260,000 light-years from the Galactic Center), with an uncertainty range of +0.53 and −0.48 × 10¹¹ solar masses. The researchers estimated a virial mass of 1.19 × 10¹² solar masses (with uncertainties of +0.14 and −0.12 × 10¹² solar masses), indicating the Milky Way has a total mass of roughly 1.2 trillion Suns. Most of this mass resides in dark matter rather than stars, gas, or dust. The measurement has implications for understanding the galaxy's formation and evolution, the behavior of satellite galaxies such as the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds, the distribution of dark matter, and predictions of when the Milky Way and Andromeda will merge.

## Quantum Computing

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Researchers Extend Qubit Lifetime by Correcting Errors Without Measurement
Researchers from Atom Computing, Microsoft Quantum, the Colorado School of Mines, and Stanford University demonstrated that reusing and replacing helper qubits (ancilla qubits) during mid-circuit computation can extend qubit lifetimes without direct measurement of data qubits. The team achieved up to 41 rounds of error detection using repetition codes while maintaining stable error rates. They showed that lost atoms could be replenished from an external trap mid-circuit without disrupting quantum coherence, and demonstrated encoded Bell state preparation with a 0.4% logical failure rate. The method integrates repeated mid-circuit measurement, atom reinitialization, and real-time atom replenishment from an external reservoir, allowing quantum circuits to extend beyond the lifespan of individual atoms. Results were posted to arXiv by the Atom Computing-led team.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> Mysterious Rotation in Cosmic Microwave Background Could Reveal New Physics
UC San Diego researchers have detected hints of a mysterious rotation pattern in the cosmic microwave background (CMB), the faint radiation left over from approximately 380,000 years after the Big Bang. The potential rotation, called cosmic birefringence, could reveal new physics beyond the Standard Model and may provide insights into dark matter and dark energy. The CMB's polarization may have rotated by a fraction of a degree over its nearly 14 billion years of travel. However, a critical challenge exists: extremely small errors in how a telescope's polarization detectors are aligned can produce a signal nearly identical to cosmic birefringence. Physicists at UC San Diego—Postdoctoral Fellow Anto I. Lonappan, Chancellor's Distinguished Professor Brian Keating, and Associate Professor Kam Arnold—developed a new technique to check how accurately different detector groups measure polarization angles. They tested their approach using observations from the European Space Agency's Planck satellite. Their findings were published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters on November 17, 2026.
