### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 8/10" aria-label="Importance 8 of 10">8</span> Scientists use lead-ink traces and X-rays to read burned ancient papyrus scrolls
Scientists at UC Berkeley discovered that lead particles in ancient ink enable X-ray imaging to reveal text in papyri carbonized by Mount Vesuvius in 79 C.E. Lead absorbed approximately 25 times more X-rays than carbonized papyrus, making written characters readable without physically unrolling fragile scrolls from the Herculaneum library.

<ul class="takeaways">
<li>Lead in ancient ink absorbs ~25 times more X-rays than carbonized papyrus</li>
<li>X-ray tomography reveals text without physically unrolling fragile scrolls</li>
<li>Published September 16, 2026 in PLOS ONE</li>
<li>Herculaneum library contains 1,000+ unreadable carbonized scrolls from antiquity</li>
</ul>

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Quantum computer simulates matter creation from early universe particle physics
Scientists led by Duke University used a 13-ion trapped-ion quantum simulator to observe string-breaking dynamics, a particle-forming process tied to early universe physics. The experiment, published September 23, 2026 in Nature Physics, simulated how connected matter building blocks separate until enough energy causes new particles to pop into existence—normally observable only in extreme conditions like the Large Hadron Collider or early universe.

<ul class="takeaways">
<li>13-ion trapped-ion quantum simulator observed string-breaking particle dynamics</li>
<li>Published September 23, 2026 in Nature Physics</li>
<li>International collaboration: Duke, University of Maryland, Oxford, Caltech, Cornell, KU Leuven</li>
<li>Two other teams separately reproduced similar physics using different quantum computing hardware</li>
</ul>
