<span class="imp imp-3" title="Importance 3/5" aria-label="Importance 3 of 5"><i class="on"></i><i class="on"></i><i class="on"></i><i></i><i></i></span> Researchers at HZDR, Wismut GmbH, and the University of Granada found that bacteria fed glycerol can convert about 95% of dissolved uranium in contaminated mine water into a stable, oxygen-resistant compound, offering a potential tool for environmental remediation.

<ul class="takeaways">
<li>Experiment used contaminated water from a flooded uranium mine in the Ore Mountains, 130-day anoxic incubation</li>
<li>Bacteria converted uranium into rare pentavalent form, forming compound FeU(V)O4</li>
<li>Compound remained stable, even increasing, when dried biomass was exposed to oxygen</li>
<li>Published in Nature Communications; further study needed on practical cleanup use</li>
</ul>

<ul class="takeaways">
<li>Published in Nature, led by Professor Zachary Wickens after five years of development</li>
<li>Free electrons attach to the first molecule encountered rather than the preferred recipient</li>
<li>Selectivity emerges after electron transfer, recycling the normally favored molecule back unchanged</li>
<li>Could enable previously inaccessible chemical coupling reactions</li>
</ul>
