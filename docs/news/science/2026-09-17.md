### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Axons may naturally resemble strings of pearls, overturning century-old textbook depiction
Johns Hopkins researchers discovered that axons naturally feature pearl-like structures (non-synaptic varicosities) rather than smooth tubes as shown in textbooks for over a century. These repeating bulges influence electrical signal transmission and brain cell signaling.

<ul class="takeaways">
<li>Study published Nature Neuroscience, December 2, 2024</li>
<li>Lead researcher: Shigeki Watanabe, Johns Hopkins associate professor</li>
<li>Pearl-like structures documented in mouse neurons and human brain tissue</li>
<li>Non-synaptic varicosities distinct from larger synaptic varicosities at neurotransmitter sites</li>
<li>Structures influence electrical signal travel; part of normal architecture, not damage</li>
</ul>

## Health

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> New experimental drug targets mesothelioma by disabling tumor antioxidant defense
Scientists developed an experimental drug that kills mesothelioma cancer cells by disabling PRX3, an antioxidant enzyme tumors depend on for survival. A phase one trial found the drug controlled disease progression in 67% of patients with relapsed mesothelioma and produced longer survival than standard treatments in critically ill patients.

<ul class="takeaways">
<li>Drug controlled disease progression in 67% of relapsed mesothelioma patients</li>
<li>Some tumors shrank; generally well tolerated</li>
<li>Works by blocking PRX3 (peroxiredoxin 3) in mitochondria</li>
<li>Mesothelioma: rare asbestos-linked cancer; ~30,000 diagnosed annually worldwide</li>
<li>Median survival without treatment ~12 months; five-year survival ~10%</li>
<li>Published in Nature Communications; sponsored phase one trial by RS Oncology, LLC</li>
</ul>

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> Hidden immune organ in skull discovered to fight brain cancer in mice
Researchers at Washington University identified previously unknown immune structures in skull bone marrow that function as rapid-response defense against brain cancer in mice. Evidence of similar immune cells in human skull bone marrow suggests the system may apply broadly, opening new treatment approaches targeting the skull's immune response.

<ul class="takeaways">
<li>Lymph node-like immune structures found in skull bone marrow</li>
<li>Act as rapid-response &#x27;security stations&#x27; against brain cancer</li>
<li>Activate before distant lymph nodes receive tumor signals</li>
<li>Contain organized immune structures including T follicular helper cells supporting antibody production</li>
<li>Evidence of similar immune cells found in human skull bone marrow</li>
<li>Strengthening local immune defense improved tumor rejection and survival in mice</li>
<li>Findings published in Nature</li>
</ul>
