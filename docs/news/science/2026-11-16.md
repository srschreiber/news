### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Ancient Viral DNA May Explain Human Brain Development Differences
Scientists at Lund University discovered that ancient viral DNA from PTERV1, an endogenous retrovirus that infected chimpanzees millions of years ago, switches off the gene LINC00662 in chimpanzees. In humans, LINC00662 remains active and appears to play an important role in early brain development. The viral DNA became embedded in the chimpanzee genome and accumulated in numerous copies across different genomic locations. These viral sequences are systematically silenced through DNA methylation, a chemical modification process that regulates gene activity. This genetic difference between humans and chimpanzees—where the same gene is active in humans but silenced in chimpanzees due to ancient viral insertion—may help explain key evolutionary differences in how human and chimpanzee brains develop. The findings were published in Science Advances by researchers including Professor Johan Jakobsson of Lund University's Department of Molecular Neurogenetics.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Physicists control magnetic spin of single atom nucleus via radio waves
Researchers at Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands have successfully controlled the magnetic spin of a nucleus inside a single atom using radio waves, a result that contradicts previous assumptions about atomic behavior. The breakthrough was achieved by simultaneously applying two separate radio wave signals to a single atom—one directed at the electron and one at the nucleus—allowing independent control of each spin. The team, led by researcher Sander Otte with lead author Hester Vennema, used a specially modified scanning tunneling microscope to deliver electrical signals to individual atoms with high precision. The nucleus's magnetic spin is significantly weaker than electron spin but better shielded from external disturbances, making it potentially valuable for quantum memory storage. The unexpected finding—that the nuclear spin could be flipped even when electron and nuclear spins were largely disentangled—challenges existing theoretical understanding of atomic magnetism. While the experiment demonstrates this control is possible, the researchers do not yet fully understand the mechanism enabling it. The findings were published in Nature Communications in November 2026.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> Microsoft outage exposes systemic risks of treating big tech as too-big-to-fail
Microsoft experienced a global outage on November 14-15, 2026, affecting more than 430 million users worldwide. The outage knocked out key services including Outlook, Teams, and Copilot, and lasted more than 18 hours before recovery began. The root cause was a small configuration change in data centres that caused the company to lose control of some computers, unable to share load across systems. When disconnected user devices automatically reconnected simultaneously, they crashed Microsoft's systems repeatedly. Microsoft faced contractual penalties potentially reaching up to $800 million per day for failed service delivery. The U.S. Department of Defense, which signed a $9.7 billion deal with Dell Federal Systems for Microsoft services in May 2026 (saving approximately $422 million annually over five years), was potentially affected, though the Pentagon did not confirm the extent of disruption. The outage highlighted systemic risks of digital infrastructure concentration: software-as-a-service business models shifted power to tech vendors over the past 20 years, with governments and businesses now heavily dependent on a small number of providers. Australia's federal police and spy agency were impacted; Indian airlines IndiGo and Air India also reported disruptions. Microsoft attributed the outage to failing automated server health checks and elevated processor usage, not a cyberattack.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> Emergency blanket material could insulate lunar habitats against extreme temperatures
Scientists at the University of Pennsylvania have developed hybrid layered insulating film stacks (HyLIFS), a multi-layered insulation material made from stacked Mylar plastic sheets that could protect lunar habitats from extreme temperatures ranging from hundreds of degrees below zero to boiling hot. The material, based on the technology used in reflective emergency blankets, comprises hundreds of extremely thin Mylar layers and maintains thermal insulation performance even after bending or being subjected to intense pressure. Unlike existing insulators such as aerogels—which are effective at heat retention but structurally fragile—HyLIFS combines thermal insulation with mechanical robustness and damage resistance, allowing it to withstand transportation, installation, accidental impacts, and years of handling. The research was published in ACS Applied Materials & Interfaces and was led by Igor Bargatin, Associate Professor in Mechanical Engineering and Applied Mechanics at Penn, and first author Danielle R. Levin, a recent Penn doctoral graduate. If manufactured at scale, the material could protect lunar habitats, spacesuits, and other equipment designed for extended use in the vacuum of space.
