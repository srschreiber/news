### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 8.5/10" aria-label="Importance 8.5 of 10">8.5</span> Breakthrough: Electron Pairs Found at Room Temperature, May Enable Superconductors
Scientists from Utrecht, Leiden, and Munich discovered that electrons can form stable pairs at much higher temperatures than previously understood—a critical requirement for superconductivity that can now occur at room temperature. The finding, published in Science, suggests that electron pairing is not the obstacle preventing room-temperature superconductivity; instead, a different mechanism is holding it back. Conventional superconductors require cooling to approximately -260°C (liquid helium temperatures). High-temperature superconductors discovered in the mid-1980s function around -173°C, but their underlying mechanism remains poorly understood and cannot be explained by the theory governing conventional superconductivity. The breakthrough indicates that achieving room-temperature superconductivity may be possible if researchers can identify and overcome the remaining barrier beyond electron pairing.

## Climate & Disaster

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 8/10" aria-label="Importance 8 of 10">8</span> 2026 Himalayan glacial collapse killed hundreds in Nepal-Tibet border region
On August 26, 2026, the lower section of the Langtang Lirung Glacier collapsed at an altitude of 5,200 meters in the Himalayan region along the Nepal-Tibet border. The glacier break triggered a cascade of ice, rock, and debris that swept down the valley floor to approximately 3,800 meters, generating massive landslides and catastrophic flash floods downstream. The collapse killed at least 162 people in Nepal according to police reports, with China's Xinhua News Agency reporting 3 dead in Tibet as of August 27. Approximately 1,000 people remained missing across both regions: Nepal reported 403 missing (including 341 foreigners), while Tibet reported 558 missing (including 260 foreigners and 2 villagers rescued). The U.S. Geological Survey determined that what was initially suspected to be an earthquake in the region was in fact seismic energy generated by the collapse itself, followed by debris flow. Satellite imagery from August 24 (before) and August 26 (after) confirmed the glacier break. The disaster occurred in Nepal's Nuwakot district and surrounding areas; communities were given little warning, with floods arriving within hours. The incident represents the second major glacial-flood event in the Lhende Khola river system within 14 months, following similar floods in August 2025 that killed 9 people.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> MIT Discovers Aging Weakens Brain's Sleep-Based Waste Clearance System
MIT researchers discovered that the brain's glymphatic system—the natural waste-removal process that occurs during sleep—becomes significantly less effective with age. The decline is linked to weaker cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) waves, which help carry waste out of the brain, as well as changes in slow electrical waves during deep sleep and altered blood flow patterns. The reduced fluid movement is not simply explained by older adults sleeping less time. Notably, some older adults retain the same powerful cleansing waves seen in younger people. The findings, published in Nature Aging with Laura Lewis as senior author and Sydney Bailes and Stephanie Williams as lead authors, suggest that weakened brain cleansing could contribute to Alzheimer's disease and dementia risk. Researchers aim to determine whether changes in fluid waves could help identify people at greater risk of cognitive decline and whether interventions could strengthen the waste-clearing process in older adults.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> SORLA Protein Shows Promise as Alzheimer's Defense
Scientists at Sanford Burnham Prebys discovered that boosting the SORLA protein (sorting-related receptor with A-type repeats) reduces toxic tau tangles and protects against Alzheimer's-related brain damage in mice. In mouse models engineered to produce additional human SORLA and develop tau tangles, increased SORLA levels reduced tau accumulation, preserved connections between brain cells (synapses), and prevented some brain shrinkage associated with neurodegeneration. Removing SORLA worsened the damage, demonstrating its critical protective role. The findings, published in Science Advances, suggest that strengthening the brain's natural protective mechanisms could lead to new treatments for Alzheimer's and other neurodegenerative diseases. SORLA was already known to help limit amyloid-beta production and accumulation; this research extends its known protective function to tau pathology, addressing both major pathological hallmarks of Alzheimer's disease.
