### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Ancient Viral DNA May Explain Human Brain Development Differences
Scientists at Lund University discovered that ancient viral DNA from PTERV1, an endogenous retrovirus that infected chimpanzees millions of years ago, switches off the gene LINC00662 in chimpanzees. In humans, LINC00662 remains active and appears to play an important role in early brain development. The viral DNA became embedded in the chimpanzee genome and accumulated in numerous copies across different genomic locations. These viral sequences are systematically silenced through DNA methylation, a chemical modification process that regulates gene activity. This genetic difference between humans and chimpanzees—where the same gene is active in humans but silenced in chimpanzees due to ancient viral insertion—may help explain key evolutionary differences in how human and chimpanzee brains develop. The findings were published in Science Advances by researchers including Professor Johan Jakobsson of Lund University's Department of Molecular Neurogenetics.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> CG Oncology's Bladder Cancer Immunotherapy Reduces Tumor Recurrence Risk by 68%
CG Oncology announced Monday, November 16, 2026, positive results from a randomized Phase 3 clinical trial of cretostimogene ("creto"), an immunotherapy for intermediate-risk bladder cancer. The trial enrolled 367 patients with localized bladder cancer. Cretostimogene is made from an engineered common cold virus designed to target cells expressing a specific mutation found in bladder tumors. The treatment is delivered directly into the bladder via catheter, where it enters mutated cancer cells, replicates, and lyses the cells. The study showed a 68% reduction in risk of tumor recurrence compared to current medical practice. Intermediate-risk bladder cancer is typically treated with minimally invasive surgery and monitored every three months; recurrence can require repeat procedures and may lead to loss of bladder function over time. The results may change clinical treatment approaches for certain bladder cancer patients.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> MIT scientists boost brain's sleep-based waste clearance with timed pink noise
MIT researchers discovered that precisely timed bursts of pink noise during sleep amplify slow electrical waves in the brain, which strengthens the natural cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) waves that clear metabolic waste. The technique involves delivering gentle sound pulses at carefully chosen moments during sleep to enhance these fluid waves—a capability the researchers say had not been demonstrated before. The findings, published in Science Translational Medicine with Laura Lewis as senior author and Joshua Levitt as lead author, suggest potential applications for improving memory, cognitive function, and potentially slowing neurodegenerative diseases associated with protein accumulation, including Alzheimer's disease. The researchers plan to test the technology in clinical populations following success in healthy adults.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> Wearable Ultrasound Patch Accelerates REM Sleep Entry Without Drugs or Surgery
University of Texas researchers have developed NEUSLeeP, a wearable ultrasound patch that stimulates deep brain regions to accelerate REM sleep entry and duration. In a study of 28 participants published in Nature Communications, the patch helped users enter REM sleep an average of 43 minutes faster and spend approximately 16 additional minutes in that stage. The soft patch attaches to skin and combines ultrasound stimulation with electrodes that monitor brain activity in real time, allowing researchers to influence sleep-related brain regions while tracking neural response. The device operates noninvasively without medication or surgery. Improvements were observed in both healthy sleepers and those with certain sleep difficulties. Volunteers reported the patch was comfortable with minimal adverse effects. The technology targets brain regions involved in REM sleep regulation using ultrasound waves at frequencies beyond human hearing to reach deep brain areas. Researchers also found preliminary evidence suggesting effects on stress regulation and brain circuits involved in emotion. The study was led by Kai Wing "Kevin" Tang, a recent UT biomedical engineering Ph.D. graduate, and supervised by Huiliang "Evan" Wang, assistant professor in the Cockrell School of Engineering's Department of Biomedical Engineering.
