### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> Microplastics Generate Toxic Reactive Molecules; New Health Risks Identified
Scientists at Columbia University and the University of California, Berkeley discovered that microplastics generate reactive oxygen species (ROS)—highly reactive and potentially damaging molecules—when they interact with water. The research, published in Nature, identifies a previously unknown chemical mechanism by which microplastics may harm human health. As plastic fragments into smaller particles, the exposed surface area increases dramatically, enabling chemical interactions with water that produce these reactive molecules. Because blood and cells contain large amounts of water, microplastics entering the human body could continue generating ROS, which can damage cellular and tissue structures. The findings offer a potential explanation for health problems linked to microplastic exposure, including heart attacks, strokes, and neurological disorders, though the study does not establish causation of specific diseases in humans. The research builds on a 2024 Columbia study that detected approximately 240,000 plastic fragments per liter of bottled water—10 to 100 times higher than previous estimates.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> AI Models May Not Replicate Human Brain Processes Despite Predicting Brain Activity
Researchers at York University discovered that artificial intelligence models can predict brain activity patterns but may rely on fundamentally different internal processes than the human brain uses. The study, led by Assistant Professor Kohitij Kar, challenged the assumption that AI systems work like the brain simply because they can successfully predict neural responses. The team developed a method called "reverse predictivity testing" to test whether the relationship between AI and brain activity works bidirectionally—if AI truly operated like the brain, brain activity should be able to predict what happens inside AI models as effectively as AI predicts brain activity. The research examined responses to 1,320 natural photographs and realistic synthetic images featuring 10 familiar subjects (bear, elephant, face, apple, car, dog, chair, plane, bird, zebra) against various backgrounds, plus 300 additional images showing the same subjects in different visual styles (outlines, drawings, simplified representations, artistic interpretations). The findings raise questions about the reliability of using these AI models as tools for understanding how the brain's visual system actually processes information, despite their widespread use in neuroscience research over the past decade.
