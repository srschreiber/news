### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Immune receptor blocking may reverse aging in mice
Stanford Medicine researchers found that blocking a single immune receptor on tissue-resident macrophages restored their ability to clear senescent neutrophils, reducing inflammation and maintaining youthful function across multiple organs in aged mice. The intervention protected mice from frailty, excess fat accumulation, heart trouble, and substantially reduced cognitive decline. The findings suggest aging may be partly driven by immune cells losing their ability to dispose of damaged, inflammation-promoting cells.

<ul class="takeaways">
<li>Blocking single immune receptor restored cellular cleanup in tissue macrophages</li>
<li>Effects observed across brain, heart, skeletal muscle, liver, spleen, kidney, and colon</li>
<li>Protected mice from frailty, excess fat, and heart trouble</li>
<li>Substantially reduced cognitive decline in aged mice</li>
<li>Published in Science with Katrin Andreasson as senior author</li>
</ul>

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Vesper bats possess unique dual antibody gene system enabling virus tolerance
Scientists at Tulane University discovered that over 500 species of vesper bats possess two separate sets of antibody genes, an arrangement never before found in any mammal. The dual heavy-chain gene system may enable bats to create a wider variety of antibodies and help explain how they tolerate viruses that cause severe disease in other animals. The finding was published in Science Advances on September 3, 2026.

<ul class="takeaways">
<li>Over 500 vesper bat species carry dual antibody gene systems</li>
<li>First discovery of dual heavy-chain gene systems in any mammal</li>
<li>May enable wider antibody variety for viral tolerance</li>
<li>Published in Science Advances on September 3, 2026</li>
<li>Led by Hannah Frank at Tulane University</li>
</ul>
