### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 8/10" aria-label="Importance 8 of 10">8</span> American Cancer Society revises prostate cancer screening guidelines to recommend routine PSA testing
The American Cancer Society updated its prostate cancer screening guidelines on November 12, 2026, recommending routine PSA (prostate-specific antigen) blood testing for average-risk men ages 50–69, with testing every two years. For high-risk groups—Black men and men with a strong family history of prostate cancer—the society now recommends yearly PSA screening starting at age 45. After age 70, screening decisions become individualized; screening should stop by age 80 or in men not expected to live another 10 years. This represents a major reversal from the 2010 guidelines, which left PSA testing for average-risk men to individual discretion after assessing cancer risk with a physician. The change reflects recent findings showing PSA screening reduces disease-specific mortality in certain populations. Routine PSA screening had previously fallen out of favor due to low specificity, leading to overdiagnosis and unnecessary follow-up procedures. The U.S. Preventive Service Task Force has not adopted the new recommendation and continues to recommend against routine population-wide PSA screening as of the date of the American Cancer Society's announcement.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Forever Chemicals Linked to Nearly Double Aggressive Breast Cancer Risk
An NIH study published in the Journal of Clinical Oncology found that women with higher blood levels of two "forever chemicals"—perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS)—had up to 93% higher rates of estrogen receptor-negative (ER-negative) breast cancer. ER-negative breast cancer is an aggressive form lacking estrogen receptors; tumor cells cannot be stimulated by estrogen to grow. Women in the highest PFOA exposure group specifically had a 67% higher rate of ER-negative breast cancer compared to women in the lowest exposure group. The study found no clear link between either chemical and overall breast cancer risk. PFOA and PFOS belong to per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), a family of chemicals used in numerous everyday consumer products and resistant to environmental breakdown. The NIH researchers examined blood samples collected before cancer diagnosis, measuring concentrations of 17 different PFAS chemicals and comparing exposure levels to subsequent breast cancer outcomes. The study was led by NIH Stadtman Investigator Alexandra White, Ph.D. HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, Jr. stated the findings "demand further investigation" and noted the role of the president of the United States's administration in pursuing evidence on environmental health risks.

## Genetics

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> All of Us study identifies thousands of new genetic variants affecting drug response
The All of Us study, conducted using data from the National Institutes of Health's All of Us program, analyzed nearly 100,000 Americans and identified thousands of new genetic variants in 16 pharmacogenes that influence responses to prescription drugs. The study, published Wednesday in Science Translational Medicine, found that every participant carried at least one variant in the pharmacogenes studied, and that one in four participants had taken a medication that could be affected by these variants. The research included participants from more diverse ancestries than previous pharmacogenomics studies. The variants affect drug metabolism and the functioning of medications including selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, statins, chemotherapy, and the blood thinner Plavix. These genetic variations can require patients to need higher or lower drug doses, experience different or worse side effects, or in some cases render drugs entirely ineffective. The study advances efforts to use genetic data to personalize drug treatments and make pharmacogenomics research applicable to a broader population.
