### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Nasal drops restore memory and prevent Alzheimer's progression in mice
University of Sydney researchers developed nasal drops using mRNA technology to restore memory and prevent Alzheimer's progression in mice. The drops stimulate brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) production, restoring youthful memory performance in aging mice and preventing cognitive decline in Alzheimer's-engineered models without removing plaques.

<li>Novel approach: effective without plaque removal</li>

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> AI analysis identifies three distinct brain types underlying ADHD and anxiety
Stanford Medicine researchers used AI to analyze thousands of functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) brain scans and identified three distinct biological brain types, or biotypes, underlying ADHD and anxiety in children. The study, published in Nature Mental Health in November 2026, examined data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study involving 11,000+ children across the United States. The analysis included 3,508 children with existing diagnoses of ADHD, anxiety, or both; 693 children who initially had neither diagnosis but subsequently developed one or both conditions; and 1,947 children who never received either diagnosis as a control group. All participants were 9 or 10 years old at enrollment and underwent fMRI scans and cognitive and behavioral assessments. Each of the three biotypes involves disruptions in different sets of brain circuits. The researchers found that children diagnosed with ADHD, anxiety, or both could belong to any of the three brain biotypes, though some patterns were more prevalent in particular diagnostic groups. The biological patterns can appear before a diagnosis is made, potentially enabling earlier detection. The findings suggest the brain differences do not neatly follow traditional symptom-based diagnostic labels, which could help doctors identify new treatment targets and determine which therapies would most benefit individual patients. Lead author was Yong Jiao, PhD, a postdoctoral scholar at the University of Iowa; co-senior authors were Yu Zhang, PhD, assistant professor of psychiatry and behavioral sciences at Stanford, and Jing Jiang, PhD, assistant professor of pediatric neurology at the University of Iowa.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> Common Sleep and Anxiety Medications May Harm Aging Brains and Increase Hospitalization Risk
Nearly one-third of older adults with dementia or other cognitive disorders take potentially harmful medications that can worsen memory, impair brain function, and increase hospitalization risk and premature death risk, according to research from the University at Buffalo published in the Journal of the American Geriatrics Society. The study analyzed data from 2,796 older adults with cognitive disorders living independently and found that exposure to potentially inappropriate medications fell from 39.3% in 2011 to 29.3% in 2022—a significant decline over the 11-year period. The medications of concern act on the central nervous system, including benzodiazepines like Xanax (used for anxiety) and sleep medications like Ambien. Potential consequences include emergency room visits, longer and more frequent hospital stays, declining mental health, and increased risk of premature death. The research team used the American Geriatrics Society's Beers Criteria, which identifies approximately 100 medications that may pose risks to older adults, and concentrated on medications presenting additional dangers for people with diagnosed cognitive disorders. The study examined a sample representing approximately 29.7 million older Americans with cognitive disorders. Researchers recommend 'deprescribing'—a medically supervised process involving removing inappropriate medications, decreasing frequency or dosage, or replacing current medications with milder alternatives—and emphasize the importance of healthcare providers working closely with community pharmacists and homecare nurses to optimize medication safety.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> Gut antibody discovered as natural colon cancer defense
Researchers at Rockefeller University discovered that immunoglobulin A (IgA), a common antibody found throughout the digestive tract, acts as a natural defense against colon cancer. In mouse studies published in Nature, mice lacking IgA developed more than twice as many colon tumors compared to mice with normal antibody levels. The protective mechanism involves IgA attaching to cells along the intestinal lining and preventing rapid cell multiplication. When researchers orally administered IgA to mice that lacked the antibody, the animals no longer showed elevated tumor development risk. The findings are relevant to people with IgA deficiency, a condition affecting approximately 1 in 500 people in the U.S., who face greater colorectal cancer risk. The research suggests a preventive approach to cancer by intervening before tumors form, rather than treating existing cancers, and reveals how gut bacteria, immune defenses, and intestinal cells interact to influence cancer development.
