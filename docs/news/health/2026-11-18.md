### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Rare Immune Disorder Breakthrough Points to New Treatment
Scientists at the Garvan Institute of Medical Research have discovered that mevalonate kinase deficiency (MKD), a rare genetic inflammatory disorder affecting hundreds of children and adults worldwide, is driven by malfunctioning natural killer (NK) immune cells rather than macrophages as previously believed for three decades. MKD causes recurring attacks of high fever, skin rashes, joint pain, and abdominal inflammation, with the most severe episodes potentially life-threatening. The researchers found that NK cells in MKD patients fail to mature properly and cannot destroy infected cells, causing them to release excessive amounts of inflammatory signaling molecules, particularly interferon gamma, which triggers widespread inflammation. The study, published in Immunity journal on November 18, 2026, points to a treatment using existing JAK inhibitor medications to neutralize interferon gamma. This approach could benefit the approximately 50 percent of MKD patients who do not respond adequately to current therapies targeting macrophage signals. One severely affected infant has already shown substantial improvement with the new treatment approach. Professor Mike Rogers, Lab Head at Garvan and senior author, described this as a new target for the inflammatory disorder that sets the stage for future clinical studies.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7.5/10" aria-label="Importance 7.5 of 10">7.5</span> Magnetic nanoparticles show promise for less-invasive Parkinson's treatment
Scientists at Friedrich-Alexander-Universität Erlangen-Nürnberg (FAU), RWTH Aachen, and the Universities of Maastricht and Leuven demonstrated that injectable magnetic nanoplatelets can stimulate deep brain regions to improve Parkinson's-like movement problems in mice without permanently implanted electrodes. The technique involves injecting specially designed magnetic nanoplatelets into a targeted brain area; when exposed to external magnetic fields, these particles generate tiny mechanical forces that activate nearby nerve cells. The approach targets the subthalamic nucleus (STN), the same region treated by conventional deep brain stimulation devices (brain pacemakers). Unlike current treatments that require surgical implantation of electrodes beneath the collarbone, the magnetic nanoparticle method uses non-implanted external magnetic fields to influence brain activity. The findings were published in Advanced Science. Researchers suggest the approach could eventually enable treatment using injectable particles and wearable magnetic headbands, potentially offering an alternative for Parkinson's patients who are not suitable candidates for or refuse conventional brain pacemaker surgery.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> MIT Discovers Aging Weakens Brain's Sleep-Based Waste Clearance System
MIT researchers discovered that the brain's glymphatic system—the natural waste-removal process that occurs during sleep—becomes significantly less effective with age. The decline is linked to weaker cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) waves, which help carry waste out of the brain, as well as changes in slow electrical waves during deep sleep and altered blood flow patterns. The reduced fluid movement is not simply explained by older adults sleeping less time. Notably, some older adults retain the same powerful cleansing waves seen in younger people. The findings, published in Nature Aging with Laura Lewis as senior author and Sydney Bailes and Stephanie Williams as lead authors, suggest that weakened brain cleansing could contribute to Alzheimer's disease and dementia risk. Researchers aim to determine whether changes in fluid waves could help identify people at greater risk of cognitive decline and whether interventions could strengthen the waste-clearing process in older adults.

### <span class="imp imp-high" title="Importance 7/10" aria-label="Importance 7 of 10">7</span> SORLA Protein Shows Promise as Alzheimer's Defense
Scientists at Sanford Burnham Prebys discovered that boosting the SORLA protein (sorting-related receptor with A-type repeats) reduces toxic tau tangles and protects against Alzheimer's-related brain damage in mice. In mouse models engineered to produce additional human SORLA and develop tau tangles, increased SORLA levels reduced tau accumulation, preserved connections between brain cells (synapses), and prevented some brain shrinkage associated with neurodegeneration. Removing SORLA worsened the damage, demonstrating its critical protective role. The findings, published in Science Advances, suggest that strengthening the brain's natural protective mechanisms could lead to new treatments for Alzheimer's and other neurodegenerative diseases. SORLA was already known to help limit amyloid-beta production and accumulation; this research extends its known protective function to tau pathology, addressing both major pathological hallmarks of Alzheimer's disease.
