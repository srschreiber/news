<div><a href="news/climate-change/2026-11-25/#world-records-six-consecutive-days-at-2c-above-preindustrial-temperatures">World records six consecutive days at 2°C above preindustrial temperatures</a>
<p>For the first time, the world experienced six consecutive days of global surface temperatures at least 2°C above preindustrial levels (baseline 1850–1900). The daily global surface temperature reached 2.24°C above preindustrial levels on Monday, November 24, 2026—the highest ever recorded—and the streak is forecast to continue for at least several more days. Previous occasions when daily temperatures exceeded 2°C occurred in November 2023 and February 2024, but neither lasted as long or reached as high. The previous single-day record was 2.05°C, set in 2023. Scientists note this six-day streak is too brief to signify the upper Paris climate agreement target has been breached, but Samantha Burgess of the Copernicus Climate Change Service described it as &quot;an exceptional global temperature anomaly&quot; and an early indication of warming to come unless fossil fuel emissions are rapidly eliminated. The temperature spikes are driven by long-term anthropogenic heating and an unusually large El Niño approaching its peak, comparable in scale to the 1870s El Niño. Zeke Hausfather of Berkeley Earth noted the new record has &quot;smashed&quot; the previous 2.05°C baseline.</p>

<div><a href="news/science/2026-11-25/#physicists-trap-and-cool-positron-to-near-absolute-zero-in-antimatter-breakthrough">Physicists Trap and Cool Positron to Near Absolute Zero in Antimatter Breakthrough</a>
<p>Physicists at Northwestern University trapped a single positron and cooled it to temperatures just above absolute zero in a wall-free, near-perfect vacuum. The positron, the antimatter counterpart of an electron, was captured from radioactive decay, slowed down, and confined in what researchers call a &quot;one-positron quantum cyclotron.&quot; Within this system, the positron behaves like an artificial atom, allowing researchers to observe and manipulate its individual quantum energy levels. The achievement enables the most precise comparison yet between matter and antimatter by measuring the positron&#x27;s magnetic properties with unprecedented accuracy against those of an electron. The Standard Model of particle physics predicts electrons and positrons should behave as nearly perfect mirror images. Any unexpected discrepancy between the two particles could expose weaknesses in the Standard Model and provide clues to why the universe contains far more matter than antimatter—a fundamental mystery since the Big Bang should have created equal amounts of both. The research was published in Physical Review Letters. Gerald Gabrielse, Board of Trustees Professor of Physics at Northwestern&#x27;s Weinberg College of Arts and Sciences and director of the university&#x27;s Center for Fundamental Physics, led the study. Thomas Myers, a Ph.D. student in Gabrielse&#x27;s laboratory, is the study&#x27;s first author.</p>
