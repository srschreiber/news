<div><a href="news/science/2026-10-06/#cern-captures-clearest-evidence-of-quark-gluon-plasma-as-primordial-liquid">CERN Captures Clearest Evidence of Quark-Gluon Plasma as Primordial Liquid</a>
<p>CERN&#x27;s Large Hadron Collider has captured the clearest evidence yet that quark-gluon plasma (QGP)—the scorching-hot matter that filled the newborn universe—behaves as a true liquid. MIT physicists leading a CERN team developed a new method to detect quarks generating wakes as they travel through the plasma, much like a duck creating ripples on water. The observations provide the first direct evidence that quark-gluon plasma responds to fast-moving particles as a unified fluid, producing waves, splashes, and swirling motion rather than behaving as a collection of independently scattering particles. The plasma is dense enough to slow down speeding quarks and respond collectively as a fluid. The research was conducted using the Compact Muon Solenoid (CMS), one of the general-purpose particle detectors at CERN, and involved members of the CMS Collaboration. By measuring wake characteristics—size, travel speed, extent, and fade duration—scientists may determine important properties of quark-gluon plasma and gain insight into how the plasma behaved during the universe&#x27;s first microseconds. Quark-gluon plasma existed in the early universe at trillions of degrees for only a few millionths of a second before cooling rapidly and allowing quarks and gluons to combine into protons, neutrons, and other particles.</p>
